Case study 01 · Retail operations

Demand forecasting & inventory planning

How much stock should a grocery store hold so shelves rarely run empty, without tying up cash?

The problem

Every week a store decides how much of each product to stock. Too little and shoppers leave empty-handed; too much and cash sits on the shelf. I asked whether a machine-learning forecast makes that decision better than the simple rules a store might use today, and by how much.

The data

  • Walmart's public M5 competition data: daily unit sales, weekly prices and a calendar of events.
  • One state and one category, California groceries: 1,437 products in 4 stores, or 5,748 product-store combinations from 2011 to 2016 (11,156,868 daily rows).
  • The last 28 days were locked away at the start and used once, for the final test.

What I did

  • Reshaped and joined the raw files in SQL (DuckDB) and profiled demand: most products sell only on some days, which shaped how I measured accuracy.
  • Built 7 simple forecasting rules a store could use (from "same as last week" to standard methods for irregular demand) and one machine-learning model (LightGBM) covering all products.
  • Compared them on 4 past periods, froze the model, then scored the locked 28 days once.
  • Turned each forecast into a weekly ordering policy with safety stock and replayed it against actual sales to measure stock held and sales lost.
  • Re-ran the simulation under 20 different assumptions for lead time, review frequency and costs.

Key findings

5.5% less stockfor the same 95% fill rate than the best simple rule ($282,159 vs $298,495 average inventory), and 10.4% less than "same weekday last week" ($315,076).
20 of 20assumption scenarios kept the same direction: 4.7% to 7.3% less stock than the best simple rule.
4.8% less errorthan the best simple rule on the locked 28 days, close to the 4.9% seen on past periods. Better than "same weekday last week" on 93% of product-stores.
10% → 93%The top 10% of product-stores carry 93% of the cost of lost sales, so the fix can be targeted.

Inventory and cost figures are simulated under stated assumptions (lead time 2 days, weekly ordering, holding and lost-sale costs set by me), not observed results.

Recommendation

Pilot the model-based forecast in a few stores, with matched control stores, and judge it on measured fill rate and average inventory. Before the pilot, fix two known weaknesses: the forecast runs 3.3% to 7.0% low each week, and safety stock should be sized from actual error patterns rather than a normal curve.

Limitations and next steps

  • One 28-day test window; the inventory simulation covers 3 weekly orders. One spring month can't show seasonality.
  • Service came in at 91.8% of order cycles against a 95% target, because irregular demand is skewed.
  • Lead times and costs are my judgement calls. With real lead times, margins and stock-out records the money figures could be quoted with confidence.
  • Holiday and price effects are associations, not proven causes. Prices in this dataset only change in weeks with sales, so price effects can't be measured cleanly.
