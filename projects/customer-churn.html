Customer churn & retention analytics

Which customers are about to stop buying, and is a retention offer worth paying for?

The problem

A UK online giftware retailer, selling mostly to small businesses, loses many customers every quarter. It wants to know which active customers are likely to stop buying in the next 90 days, why, and whether a retention offer would pay for itself.

The data

  • UCI Online Retail II: 1,067,371 invoice lines from December 2009 to December 2011.
  • After removing duplicates, invalid prices and lines with no customer ID: 794,183 lines and 5,875 customers.
  • "Churned" means an active customer made no purchase in the 90 days after a given date.

What I did

  • Cleaned the transactions with every rule written down, and built monthly cohorts to see how many new customers come back.
  • Built 21 behavioural features (how often, how recently and how much each customer buys) using only data from before each cutoff date, with a test proving no future data leaks in.
  • Compared a simple "days since last purchase" rule, logistic regression and gradient boosting. All were tuned on earlier months, frozen, then tested once on a later month (September 2011).
  • Turned the risk scores into a costed contact list and re-ran it across 27 combinations of margin, save rate and offer cost.
  • Checked the plan after the fact against what customers actually did.

Key findings

83.1% vs 73.7%Of the 10% of customers the model rated riskiest, 360 of 433 did stop buying, against 73.7% for the "days since last purchase" rule (50.5% churned overall).
£22,621expected net benefit from contacting 1,567 customers in the central case (estimate: 30% margin, 10% save rate, £15 offer).
21 to 4,212customers: the best list size across the 27 scenarios. The answer depends far more on the assumptions than on the model.
£7,473The same plan recomputed with what customers actually did. Still positive, but well below the estimate.

£ figures are estimates under stated assumptions. Nothing here shows that an offer causes anyone to stay. The strongest warning signs (few orders and low spend in the past year, being a newer customer, a long gap since the last purchase) are associations, not causes.

Recommendation

Use the score to rank customers, not to forecast how many will leave. Before a full rollout, run a small controlled campaign: send the offer to a random half of the chosen list to measure the real save rate. That one number decides how far down the list it pays to go.

Limitations and next steps

  • Margin, save rate and offer cost are assumptions; only a controlled campaign can measure the save rate.
  • A kept customer is valued at 12 months of revenue while "churned" means only 90 days without buying, so benefits are likely overstated.
  • Churn was lower in the test month (50.5%) than in training (61.3% to 67.0%), possibly pre-Christmas buying. One autumn of data can't separate season from trend.
  • One retailer, 2009–2011 data, and a few large wholesale accounts dominate the £ totals (top 1% of customers hold 29.0% of revenue).
